How Overtime is Taxed in the UK

Overtime income is not subject to any punitive penalty rate. Instead, it is added to your total pay and taxed at your current marginal rate of Income Tax and National Insurance.

Marginal Tax Rates

If your regular salary is within the 20% basic band, overtime pay is taxed at 20% Income Tax + 8% NI (keeping £72 per £100).

Higher Rate Bracket

If overtime pushes total annual pay above £50,270, the excess is taxed at 40% Income Tax + 2% NI (keeping £58 per £100).

Holiday Pay Inclusion

Under UK employment law, regular overtime must be included when calculating your statutory holiday pay over a 52-week reference period.

Frequently Asked Questions

Clear answers to common questions about UK overtime pay.

Overtime is not subject to special tax rates; it is simply added to your cumulative income for the pay period. If the extra pay pushes your total income over a higher rate threshold (£50,270 for 40% tax), only the amount exceeding the threshold is taxed at the higher rate.

Employers can only require overtime if it is explicitly stated in your employment contract. Even then, your average working hours cannot exceed 48 hours per week unless you have signed a voluntary opt-out agreement.

Time and a half means you are paid 1.5 times your standard hourly rate (e.g. £20/hr becomes £30/hr). Double time means you are paid twice your standard hourly wage (e.g. £20/hr becomes £40/hr), often used for working Sundays or public bank holidays.

Salaried employees only receive overtime pay if specified in their contract. However, unpaid overtime must never cause average pay to drop below the statutory National Living Wage.
